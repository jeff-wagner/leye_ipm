Description:
Abstract:
This data release contains tabular data pertaining to population surveys of shorebirds, predatory birds (i.e., raptors, cranes, and gulls), and other bird species (i.e., passerines) conducted across the Upper Cook Inlet region of Alaska during 1996 and 1998. The data package includes five tables: 1) characteristics of bird survey sites including environmental conditions and habitat classifications, 2) behaviors and habitat associations of all shorebirds and avian predatory species detected at each survey site, 3) a list of all birds species (i.e., passerines, shorebirds, predatory birds, etc.) detected at each survey site, 4) definitions of habitat classification codes, and 5) bird species codes and taxonomy. Additionally, shapefiles for each point count location are also included in this data release.
Purpose:
The project objective was to characterize breeding bird communities across the Upper Cook Inlet region of Alaska. The surveys have/will be repeated in 2026 and beyond. The current objectives are to detect changes in bird species distributions, abundances, and demography overtime in response to projected environmental changes in the Upper Cook Inlet region of Alaska.

Process_Step:
Process_Description:
METHODS: Site Selection
We conducted fieldwork from late April to early-July, 1996 and 1998, at sites within the Anchorage Bowl and on or near the Susitna Flats State Game Refuge. We surveyed 5 inland sites and 3 intertidal sites at Anchorage and 3 inland sites, 1 supratidal site, and 2 intertidal sites at Susitna Flats. We characterized sites by their primary habitat type (i.e., tidal flats, salt marsh, shrub bog, shrub-spruce bog, wet mixed forest), tidal regime, distance to nearest bog and shore (for measures of connectedness and isolation, respectively), and features of the surrounding landscape.

Process_Step:
Process_Description:
METHODS: Point Count Surveys
In 1996, we sampled a series of 49 points (located along 6 routes) in the Susitna Flats and 35 points (6 routes) in Anchorage once every 7-10 days between 24 April and 7 July. In 1998, we sampled the same 35 points in Anchorage plus an additional 9 points (2 routes) once every 7-9 days between 30 April and 3 July. Points were located 500 m apart along non-random survey routes that distributed points across study sites. Many routes followed infrequently-traveled gravel roads (all inland routes at Susitna Flats) and footpaths (1 intertidal and 3 inland routes at Anchorage). Other routes were located cross-country (1 intertidal site in the Susitna Flats, 2 intertidal and 2 inland routes in Anchorage).
Surveys were conducted on randomly chosen days and daylight periods (morning 0600-1200 hrs, afternoon 1200-1800, evening 1800-2400).
Immediately before each count, we estimated wind speed and direction, and noted general weather conditions (rain, etc.). Observers spent 10 minutes at each sample point listening and visually scanning (with the aid of binoculars) for birds. For each detection of shorebirds and potential shorebird predators, the following information was recorded: elapsed time; species; number in flock; microhabitat (e.g., Triglochin marsh pond, intertidal flats puddle, shrub-bog); behavior (e.g., flying, foraging, swimming, displaying); vocalization (calling, singing, alarm-calling); type of detection cue (audio, visual); and approximate radial distance from point (meters). The primary habitat within a 50-meter radius of the survey point was classified using U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service National Wetlands Inventory (NWI) categories.
